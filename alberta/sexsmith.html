Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, the permitting line is mostly about structural impact. Structural concrete work—such as footings, foundation walls, grade beams and other elements that support a building—generally requires a building permit and often engineered drawings reviewed by the municipality. Concrete flatwork (patios, walkways and typical driveways) usually does not require a permit unless it affects lot grading, drainage, or impervious surface coverage rules, or encroaches near a municipal right-of-way. Always confirm with the City/County planning or development office before breaking ground, especially if you’re changing elevations or redirecting surface water.

For compliance, start by verifying that your contractor carries liability insurance and Workers’ Compensation coverage (WSIB/WCB). Then ask for references on similar-scale concrete projects in Alberta weather conditions—driveways, steps, and foundation-related work. Step-by-step for Sexsmith homeowners:

  • Ask for the contractor’s business address and project references (similar pour season and exterior exposure).
  • Request a certificate of liability insurance naming you/your property correctly (if applicable) and confirm the coverage is current.
  • Confirm WSIB/WCB status with the documentation the contractor provides (or via the provider clearance approach they use).
  • For structural work, confirm the contractor will coordinate the engineering/permit documents before the pour.
  • If permits are triggered, ensure the permit is pulled before excavation and placement—not after.

If you’re ever unsure, it’s worth asking directly: “Is my scope flatwork only, or does it include structural concrete elements that require a permit?”

Getting concrete right in Sexsmith's freeze-thaw climate

Concrete tends to fail in Sexsmith when the foundation and slab system are built for the “nice weather” scenario instead of for freeze–thaw cycles, moisture movement and de-icing salt. The most common problems I see are scaling and surface spalling on exterior flatwork, settlement-driven cracking, and random cracks that appear early because the base and joint plan weren’t matched to the load and exposure. In the Athabasca–Grande Prairie–Peace River region, the solution is to spec the whole system: base, mix, joints, curing and sealing.

(1) Base preparation: place and compact granular sub-base to the correct depth and grade for local soil and frost conditions. If water can move beneath a slab, freeze–thaw can lift the edge and break the finish. (2) Mix design: specify air-entrainment and exterior durability. A common target is 5–7% air content for exterior flatwork and a minimum 32 MPa compressive strength (as specified/engineered for the use). (3) Control joints: design the joint spacing so cracks start where you want them, not randomly. (4) Curing: plan for minimum 7 days of proper curing conditions; in cold weather, curing may require heated enclosures and insulated blankets to reach strength safely. (5) Sealing: apply a penetrating sealer after the slab has cured—typically at 28 days—to repel water and de-icing salt intrusion.

Here’s where the price difference is (and isn’t) justified: upgrading from plain broom-finish concrete ($14–$20 per sq. ft. tier for driveways/slabs) to stamped concrete ($22–$35 per sq. ft.) isn’t only about looks—stamped work benefits from better curing discipline and careful finish timing. But if the contractor is cutting corners on air-entrainment or base prep, you can’t “buy your way out” of spalling risk later.

When you quote the work, ask technical questions about air entrainment, joint plan, curing method and the exact base depth—because those are the levers that stop exterior concrete from deteriorating through Sexsmith winters.

Frequently asked questions — concrete work in Sexsmith

What causes concrete to spall in Sexsmith winters?

Spalling in Sexsmith typically comes from moisture getting into the concrete and then freezing repeatedly. If the exterior slab or walkway doesn’t have proper air entrainment (and the right curing), water can expand and break the surface paste. Poor drainage and weak base prep can also trap water at slab edges, which accelerates freeze–thaw damage. De-icing salt makes the situation worse because it increases salt exposure at the surface; over time, that drives scaling and flaking. If your slab was poured with a low-quality mix, insufficient air content, or rushed curing in cold weather, you can see early surface breakdown even if the concrete looks fine when first poured. In practical terms, ask your contractor what air content target they’re using and whether the job includes curing blankets/heating when it’s cold.

What is rebar and when is it required in Alberta?

Rebar is steel reinforcement used inside concrete to help control cracking and increase strength, especially where slabs or structural elements experience tension. In Alberta, whether rebar is required depends on the design and load conditions—driveways for heavier vehicles, thickened edges, and interior/exterior slabs that carry stress may call for wire mesh or rebar. For structural concrete like footings and foundation elements below frost, reinforcement requirements are typically determined by engineered drawings and the structural design. For homeowners in Sexsmith, the key is to ask your contractor whether your specific scope calls for mesh or rebar, and to see how it’s placed (not just that it “exists”). For footings below frost, it’s common that reinforcement is specified for the structural system rather than “optional.”

How long does a concrete project take in Sexsmith?

Timelines in Sexsmith depend on weather, site access and curing conditions. For many exterior flatwork jobs—say a small patio or walkway—excavation and forming may take a day or two, placing and finishing is usually one day, and curing time affects when you can use it. Even though a slab gains handling strength quickly, the durability expectations align with proper curing; many contractors plan at least 7 days before normal use, and sealing is often targeted around 28 days post-pour for best results. Driveway replacements can take longer due to heavier excavation, base preparation and proper joint layout. In colder periods, cold-weather curing precautions (insulation blankets/heating) can add time and labour. If you’re comparing quotes, ask for a start date, completion estimate, and how curing will be managed given the pour day forecast.

What is a concrete overlay and is it suited to Sexsmith?

A concrete overlay is a new concrete layer placed over an existing slab (after prep), meant to restore appearance and levelness without full removal. In Sexsmith, overlays can be suitable if the existing concrete is still structurally sound—no significant slab movement, minimal deterioration, and a surface that can be properly prepared for bonding. However, overlays can fail if the original slab has heave, settlement, or moisture issues because the new layer can crack or debond in step with the old problem. That’s why thorough assessment matters: check for spalling, drainage issues, and joint failure. If your driveway is already scaling from freeze–thaw, sometimes removal and re-pour is the better long-term option. As a budgeting reference, plain concrete patio/walkway work often sits around the lower bands (for example, the $12–$18 per sq. ft. tier for patios/walkways), while overlays may not always deliver proportional savings if prep is extensive.

How do I seal concrete to protect it in Sexsmith?

Sealing helps in Sexsmith because it reduces how quickly water and de-icing salts penetrate the surface, which slows scaling and surface wear in freeze–thaw cycles. The important part is timing: apply penetrating sealer after the slab has cured sufficiently—often around 28 days post-pour—so you don’t trap moisture inside. Before sealing, the concrete should be clean, dry, and free of contaminants; contractors typically pressure wash and allow drying time. For exterior driveways and steps, choose a penetrating sealer designed for salt exposure rather than a simple decorative coating. Ask what product is used and whether the contractor tests moisture or waits for weather conditions suitable for sealing. If your scope includes sealing, make sure it’s specified in the contract—some driveway quotes focus on the pour only, and sealing can be a meaningful add-on.

What is the difference between concrete and cement?

Cement is one component of concrete. Concrete is the complete engineered mix: cement (which acts as a binder) plus aggregates (sand and gravel), water, and—when required for exterior exposure—admixtures like air-entrainment. In Sexsmith, the distinction matters because “using cement” is not the same as specifying the correct exterior concrete performance. For example, exterior flatwork needs air entrainment and curing discipline to handle freeze–thaw. The broader durability spec is about the full concrete mixture and placement method, not just the brand of cement. When you get a quote, ask what the mix design targets (air content for exterior, compressive strength such as 32 MPa, and curing approach). This is also why two driveways with similar sizes can vary by 30–50%: the contractor isn’t only changing material brand—they’re changing mix design, base thickness, and cold-weather handling to match Athabasca–Grande Prairie–Peace River conditions.
