Concrete work options and costs in London

Concrete work in London, Ontario is a big part of everyday home maintenance and upgrades—especially because so many properties are built around established lots. In London, single-detached homes make up 48.8% of dwellings, and that’s exactly the market where driveways, patios, walkways, and garage floors get installed or replaced. With 101,555 homeowner households (58.1% of all households owning), there’s strong demand for durable exterior flatwork that can handle local freeze–thaw cycles without failing early. (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census.)

Pricing typically reflects a few London-specific cost drivers: frost depth requirements that push footings and some slab edges deeper than homeowners expect, and the reality that repeated winter cycles can destroy poorly prepared bases. Contractors in the London area (including around neighbourhoods like Westmount and the South London corridor) also price for schedule availability and the practical need to time pours to get quality finishing and curing. In cold, damp Southwestern Ontario weather, curing protection—blankets, tenting, or heaters when temperatures drop—adds labour and materials, particularly for exterior flatwork and decorative finishes.

Because all of these factors change what’s “under” the concrete (excavation, granular base, reinforcement, and joints), two quotes for the same visible surface can still be 30–50% apart. The table below uses typical, locally realistic price bands to compare options.

What affects the price of concrete work in London

In London, Ontario, the same “concrete slab” description can generate quotes that differ by about 30–50% because contractors are pricing different depths, different site conditions, and different durability specs. The biggest drivers are frost line depth, freeze–thaw exposure, and how well the base and drainage are engineered before any concrete is placed. For Southwestern Ontario, footings and any exterior slab elements that must resist frost movement are typically designed to extend below the local design frost depth (commonly in the 1.5–2.4 m range across Ontario). That depth requirement dictates excavation volume, formwork height, reinforcement quantity, and concrete tonnage—so it doesn’t just affect the price; it changes the whole build.

Freeze–thaw also dictates air-entrained concrete for exterior flatwork. Where pricing lands near the low end of typical patio/walkway ranges (around $12–$22 per m²), you’ll often see fewer upgrades. When you move toward the mid-to-high end (closer to driveway and decorative bands like $15–$25 for driveways and $22–$40 for stamped/decorative), you usually get better base preparation, tighter control joint planning, and cold-weather curing precautions (blankets/heating and scheduling). De-icing salt use around London increases surface wear, so sealing becomes more than a cosmetic add-on—it's durability insurance.

Two common London examples: (1) A backyard patio poured over poorly drained topsoil tends to cost more because it requires extra granular excavation and regrading to create a stable base; (2) A stamped driveway often costs more because the schedule and finishing steps are more labour-intensive—while a plain broom finish may be faster but still depends on correct joints and curing to avoid scaling and cracking. Homes built before 1981 make up 52.3% of the housing stock, and many of these properties have older drainage patterns that need correction during replacement work—one reason foundation and exterior projects can sit on the higher side of local bands.

Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, structural concrete work—things tied directly to the building’s structural system—typically requires a building permit, and often engineered drawings that are reviewed through the municipal permitting process. That includes foundation walls, grade beams, footings, and similar load-bearing elements. Concrete flatwork (like patios, walkways, and driveways) usually does not require a permit by default, unless the work affects lot grading, stormwater/impervious surface rules, or ties into municipal right-of-way concerns (for example, drainage patterns and how water is managed near curb/sidewalk areas). Always confirm with the local authority before breaking ground.

Homeowners in London should also treat contractor compliance as part of the project plan, not an afterthought. Step-by-step, verify that the contractor carries:

  • Liability insurance (request a current certificate of insurance naming you if required by the contract)
  • WSIB/WCB coverage (for Ontario, ask for proof of registration/clearance)
  • Any required licensing for the contracting entity for the type of work being performed
  • References for similar projects in the London/area market, ideally with photos of exterior flatwork after a winter

Where to look: use the contractor’s public business presence/registry record for licence status (search their legal business name), then review the certificate of insurance and any WSIB/WCB proof you’re provided. Finally, ask for written confirmation of what is included—permit support (if required), excavation disposal, and curing protection—so there are no surprises once cold weather arrives.

Getting concrete right in London's freeze-thaw climate

Concrete failures in London usually come from three places: weak or improperly compacted base, the wrong mix for exterior freeze–thaw exposure, and curing that isn’t protected through cold nights and rain. If base material is not compacted to the right depth and slope for drainage, water sits under the slab. When it freezes, it can lead to settlement, rocking, and ultimately cracking. If the mix isn’t air-entrained, freeze–thaw can cause surface scaling and spalling—often the first visible sign homeowners notice.

To spec the job correctly for London conditions, focus on five technical items. First, base preparation: use a compacted granular sub-base to the correct thickness for the slab type and local soil conditions, with drainage that helps water move away from the slab edges. Second, mix design: for exterior flatwork, specify air-entrainment (commonly 5–7% air content for freeze–thaw durability) and minimum compressive strength (often 32 MPa for typical exterior applications). Third, control joints: plan joint spacing to manage cracking, rather than letting the concrete crack randomly. Fourth, curing: require at least 7 days of proper curing conditions—especially in cooler, damp Southwestern Ontario weather—so it reaches design strength before it’s exposed to freeze cycles. Fifth, sealing: apply a penetrating sealer after cure (commonly around 28 days) to help repel de-icing salts and reduce surface deterioration.

A practical pricing example: upgrading from a plain broom-finish driveway to a stamped finish isn’t just “looks.” If the quote is, say, around $15,000–$25,000 for a standard driveway but $22,000–$40,000 for stamped, the difference is typically the stamp/colour system, more labour time for finishing, and often tighter scheduling to keep colour and texture consistent—while still depending on the same durable base and joint plan. If the base and curing are weak, you can spend more and still get early cracking, so ask for mix, joints, and curing details—every time.

How to choose a concrete work contractor in London

Choosing the right concrete contractor in London means verifying compliance, comparing quotes properly, and making sure the scope matches the real risks of freeze–thaw. Start by confirming Ontario requirements. Ask for proof of liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage (request documents, not verbal assurance). For insurance, review the certificate of insurance and confirm it’s current. For WSIB/WCB, ask for the clearance letter or proof of registration status—contractors who can’t provide it usually mean you’re taking on risk. Then verify their business details and contracting credentials through public online registry listings using their legal business name.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown by labour, concrete supply, base prep, reinforcement, excavation, disposal, joints, and finish materials (especially for stamped or exposed aggregate). Avoid “lump sum” quotes that don’t explain depth, reinforcement, or how curing and sealing will be handled. Read the exclusions: Is permit pulling included for structural work? Is disposal of excess soil part of the price? Are cutting and joint sealing included, and are they scheduled?

Warranty should be specific. Ask for a workmanship warranty length and what it covers (cracking due to shrinkage vs. settlement due to base failures). Also ask about manufacturer warranties for sealers or decorative systems and whether they are transferable if you sell your home.

For payment schedule, don’t allow large upfront payments; keep initial deposits within 10–15% and hold back until key items are complete and inspected. Finally, require a start date and completion estimate in writing—London’s seasonal temperatures can affect when good curing protection is possible.

  • Request mix details (air-entrainment and strength), not just “concrete”
  • Confirm slab thickness and base thickness in writing
  • Ask how they handle drainage around edges and downspout discharge
  • Verify rebar/mesh spec and where it’s placed (chairs/tickets for cover)
  • Require a control joint plan (spacing and saw-cut timing)
  • Confirm curing protection method for cool nights (blankets/heating if required)
  • Ask about finishing schedule to avoid surface defects
  • Get sealant details if included (type and application timing)
  • Confirm excavation disposal and hauling is included or clearly excluded
  • Check references with projects in the London area, preferably exterior flatwork
  • Ensure lien and warranty terms are in the contract
  • Insist on permits/engineering requirements for structural items—clarify who submits what

London concrete red flags: no written mix/air requirement, vague “standard base” language without compaction details, refusing to discuss control joints, skipping cold-weather curing plans, and asking for large upfront payments before excavation or reinforcement inspection.

Frequently asked questions — concrete work in London

Concrete vs pavers for a patio in London?

In London, both concrete and pavers can perform well, but freeze–thaw changes how each system fails. Concrete patios (properly air-entrained and jointed) tend to age more uniformly; when they crack, control joints usually manage where that happens. Pavers can be great when you want easy access for repairs to drainage or utilities, but they rely heavily on a well-compacted base and correct edging to prevent shifting during winter cycles. If you’re price-sensitive, plain concrete patios typically land around $12–$22 per m², while pavers can vary widely based on the unit and base build. For a similar area, a well-specified concrete bid may look higher upfront, but fewer interlock movement issues can mean less maintenance. Ask both contractors what they’re doing for drainage slope and base depth.

How long does a concrete driveway last?

A concrete driveway in London often lasts 20–30 years when the contractor gets the base, mix design, and joints right—especially because de-icing salt and freeze–thaw cycles are hard on exterior surfaces. The biggest predictors of lifespan are whether the concrete is air-entrained, whether the granular base is properly compacted, and whether joints were placed and cut on the right schedule. If a driveway is done with poor drainage, water gets trapped, freezes, and pushes the slab. Also, skipping sealing can increase scaling over time. If your quote is in the typical driveway band (about $15,000–$25,000 for many standard-size projects), compare what’s included: base depth, reinforcement, joint plan, and curing protection. A slightly higher quote is often justified when it includes better specs rather than just thicker-looking concrete.

What is air-entrained concrete and why does it matter here?

Air-entrained concrete is mixed with tiny, intentionally formed air bubbles that help the cement paste tolerate freeze–thaw expansion. In London, Ontario, winter brings repeated freezing and thawing, and that cycling is what drives surface scaling and spalling when the air content is wrong or the mix isn’t designed for exterior exposure. This is one reason London pricing depends on more than thickness: the mix design and quality control matter. A reputable contractor will specify air-entrainment targets (often 5–7% air content for exterior flatwork) and a strength specification (commonly 32 MPa or better, depending on the application). If you hear only “we use concrete” without mix details, you should ask follow-up questions. Proper air-entrainment, combined with curing and sealing, is one of the simplest ways to protect your driveway or patio across winters.

How do I prepare the base for a concrete slab?

For a concrete slab in London, base preparation is about drainage control and compaction quality. Your contractor should excavate to the required depth, remove unsuitable material (like topsoil and organic layers), and build a compacted granular base with the correct thickness for the slab type. You want clean, properly graded material and compaction to the level needed to limit settlement. The goal is to prevent water from pooling under the slab edges—since freeze–thaw can turn moisture into movement. Ask what granular material is proposed, what the compaction method will be (plate compactor, roller, test results if available), and how slope/drainage is handled. If your site has poor drainage or a history of standing water, base work can increase the cost, but it’s often the difference between a slab that looks fine for a year and one that survives multiple winters.

How do I compare concrete quotes?

When comparing quotes in London, Ontario, focus on what’s under the concrete and how it’s cured—not just the final price. Ask each contractor for an itemised breakdown: excavation depth, granular base thickness and compaction, reinforcement type and placement, concrete mix details (air-entrainment and strength), finish plan, control joint spacing, curing protection, and whether sealing is included. Make sure the finish is described clearly—plain broom versus stamped versus exposed aggregate—and confirm who supplies specialty materials. If one bid is close to the typical driveway band (for example $15,000–$25,000 for standard driveway work) and another is much higher, it should come with extra durability steps like cold-weather curing measures or better base engineering. Be cautious about bids that omit jointing, curing protection, or base compaction details; those omissions can show up as cracking or scaling during the next freeze–thaw season.

Can concrete be poured in cold weather in London?

Concrete can be poured in cold weather in London, but it needs the right protection to achieve strength and durability. The key issue isn’t just “can we place it”—it’s whether curing happens under controlled temperatures so the concrete reaches design strength before it’s exposed to damaging freeze–thaw cycles. Contractors may use heating, insulating blankets, and schedule adjustments, and they often monitor temperatures through finishing and curing. If a quote assumes a quick, unprotected pour during cold nights, that’s a risk for scaling and early surface damage on exterior flatwork. Ask the contractor what cold-weather curing plan they’ll use, including blankets/heaters and how long they’ll maintain protection. For exterior jobs with de-icing salt exposure, sealing timing matters too (often after curing, around the 28-day mark). A responsible approach can still work, but it should be planned and documented.
