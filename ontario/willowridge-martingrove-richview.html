Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, the rule of thumb is that structural concrete work needs more oversight than decorative or surface-only flatwork. For example, structural concrete such as footings, foundation walls, and grade beams typically requires a building permit, and engineered drawings are often reviewed by the municipality as part of the approval process. If you’re changing the structure, supporting loads, or altering foundation elements, assume permits apply.

Concrete flatwork—like patios, walkways, and driveways—typically does not require a permit on its own, unless it affects lot grading, touches impervious surface coverage limits, or involves work near a municipal right-of-way. Even when permits aren’t required for the slab, contractors still need to ensure drainage and elevation meet local requirements so runoff doesn’t create problems.

To verify a contractor in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview, follow this homeowner checklist: (1) confirm the company’s business credentials and any applicable provincial registration, (2) request a certificate of insurance showing general liability, (3) ask for WSIB/WCB clearance or coverage proof for their workers, and (4) confirm they can provide references on similar-scale concrete work. Step-by-step, you’ll look for the insurance certificate that matches the contracting entity name, and you’ll obtain WSIB/WCB confirmation directly from the proof letter or clearance documentation before any excavation starts.

Getting concrete right in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview's freeze-thaw climate

Concrete failure in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview is usually less about “bad concrete” and more about a missing piece of the system under our freeze–thaw cycles. The two most common problems I see are spalling (surface scaling from winter salt and freezing) and cracking from movement (insufficient base, missing or mis-spaced joints, or poor drainage). To spec the job correctly, start with base prep: use a compacted granular sub-base to the right depth for local soils and frost protection, and build in proper slopes so water drains away rather than saturating the underside.

Next, require a mix design that can survive winter. For exterior flatwork, that means air-entrainment (typically a target air content in the 5–7% range for many exterior mixes) and a minimum compressive strength such as 32 MPa where specified for flatwork. Then plan for control: joints aren’t optional—spacing governs where shrinkage and thermal cracking happens. Curing is critical too: protect and cure for at least 7 days under proper conditions, and if you’re pouring when evenings get cold, additional insulation or heating plans may be needed to keep strength gain on track. Finally, apply a penetrating sealer after cure—commonly around 28 days—to help repel de-icing salts and reduce surface deterioration.

Here’s where the dollars make sense: a properly prepped base and correct air-entrained mix might cost more upfront, but it avoids the cycle of patching and premature replacement. For instance, moving from a basic driveway build toward a higher-spec exterior plan can still fall within the typical driveway band $18,000–$28,000, while repeated “cheap patch” repairs often cost far more over 3–5 winters. Ask the contractor to show how they’ll meet air-entrainment and joint spacing so you’re not betting on the weather.

How to choose a concrete work contractor in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview

Choosing the right contractor in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview is mostly about verification and clarity. Start with Ontario requirements and basic risk checks: ask for their general liability insurance certificate (so you’re not left holding the bag for damage or injury), and confirm they have WSIB/WCB coverage for their workers. For Ontario licensing, don’t rely on verbal claims—ask for the business details they’re registered under and the specific documentation they can provide (and then match it to the insurance certificate’s legal name).

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes, not lump-sum guesses. A good quote separates labour and materials (excavation, forms, base, reinforcement, concrete supply, finishing, and sealing). It should also state what’s excluded: permit fees (if applicable), disposal/hauling of existing material, geotextile or drainage features, and protection for curing in cooler weather. Read the scope like a contract—if you can’t find the curing or base prep details, you’re likely buying “minimum compliance.”

Warranty is also important in concrete because Ontario winters expose defects fast. Ask for a workmanship warranty length, whether any product/manufacturer warranty is included, and whether the warranty is transferable if you sell your home. For payment, avoid large deposits—keep upfront payments around 10–15% and hold back money until the job is complete and cleaned up. Finally, request a written start date and a completion estimate that accounts for curing and weather protection—this is where Toronto-area scheduling differs from milder regions.

How long does a concrete project take in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview?

What is a concrete overlay and is it suited to Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview?

A concrete overlay is a new layer of concrete placed on top of an existing slab, usually after patching spalls and properly preparing the surface (often including grinding, cleaning, and applying a suitable bonding system). In Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview, overlays can work when the original slab is largely stable, well-drained, and not experiencing major structural movement. If the base is failing, water is undermining the slab, or the existing concrete is heaving with freeze–thaw, an overlay can crack and spall again—sometimes faster than a full rebuild. Cost-wise, overlays can be a middle ground, but they don’t fix the underlying moisture or base issues. If you’re seeing widespread cracking or repeating patches, you may be better budgeting for a full driveway or patio rebuild in the typical range such as $18,000–$28,000 for many standard driveway projects.

How do I seal concrete to protect it in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview?

Sealing helps protect exterior concrete surfaces from water and de-icing salt—both are big drivers of winter scaling in Ontario. The key is timing: allow adequate curing first. A common approach is applying a penetrating sealer after the concrete has cured—often about 28 days—so moisture levels are reasonable and the sealer can penetrate rather than trap water. Before sealing, the surface should be clean and dry; contractors typically power wash and allow full drying. Ask for the sealer product type (penetrating versus film-forming) and ensure it’s compatible with air-entrained exterior concrete. Keep in mind that sealing isn’t a cure-all for poor base prep or missing air-entrainment. It supports durability, but a correctly built slab with proper drainage is still what prevents repeated freeze–thaw damage.

What is the difference between concrete and cement?

People often use “cement” and “concrete” interchangeably, but there’s a real difference. Cement is one component inside concrete—it’s the binding material that reacts with water to create strength. Concrete is the full mixture: cement plus water plus aggregate (sand and stone), and for most exterior work in Ontario, an air-entrainment system is included to manage freeze–thaw performance. When a contractor quotes concrete flatwork, you should ask about the mix design: air content targets for exterior slabs and the minimum compressive strength (commonly 32 MPa in many specs for residential durability). In Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview, the freeze–thaw cycle makes air-entrainment and curing practices particularly important, because surface scaling can accelerate if the cement/aggregate system isn’t properly designed.
